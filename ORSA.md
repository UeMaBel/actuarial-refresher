# ORSA

> ORSA is the insurer’s own forward-looking assessment of its risks, capital needs, and ability to remain solvent while pursuing its business plan.

**ORSA — Own Risk and Solvency Assessment**

It belongs to Pillar II.

## Core question

Can we carry out our strategy and continue meeting our obligations, including under adverse conditions?

ORSA connects:

- Business strategy
- Risk profile
- Financial projections
- Capital planning
- Management decisions

It is an ongoing management process, not just a regulatory report.

## Three required assessments

### 1. Overall solvency needs

How much capital and other resources does the insurer need, considering its specific risks, risk appetite, and business strategy?

This assessment can include risks not fully captured by the regulatory capital calculation, such as liquidity, strategic, or reputational risks.

### 2. Continuous compliance

Can the insurer continue meeting its regulatory capital requirements and technical-provision requirements as its business develops?

This requires looking forward rather than checking only today’s balance sheet.

### 3. Deviations from SCR assumptions

Does the insurer’s actual risk profile differ significantly from the assumptions underlying its SCR calculation?

For example, the standard formula may not adequately reflect an unusual concentration or a particular product feature.

## ORSA versus SCR

| SCR | ORSA |
|---|---|
| Regulatory capital requirement | Insurer-specific assessment process |
| One-year risk calibration | Forward-looking business-planning perspective |
| Standard formula or approved internal model | Methods appropriate to the insurer’s risks |
| Produces a capital figure | Supports decisions about strategy, capital, and risk |

ORSA does not automatically create an additional regulatory capital requirement.

## Typical process

1. Identify material risks.
2. Assess whether existing models capture them adequately.
3. Project the business plan and capital position.
4. Run adverse scenarios and sensitivity analyses.
5. Evaluate possible management actions.
6. Use the findings in business decisions.
7. Document and communicate the assessment.

The management body is responsible for overseeing and challenging the process.

## Scenarios and stress testing

Real-World Scenarios can project plausible economic outcomes.

An Economic Scenario Generator can supply paths for interest rates, equities, inflation, and other financial variables.

**Stress testing:** What happens under a specified adverse event?

**Reverse stress testing:** What circumstances could make the business model fail?

Examples:

- Prolonged low investment returns
- A sharp interest-rate increase combined with high surrenders
- Claims inflation exceeding pricing assumptions
- An adverse mortality or morbidity trend
- Reduced access to capital or liquidity

## Example

An insurer currently has an SCR coverage ratio of 180%.

Its growth plan requires substantial new business investment. Under an adverse scenario, projected coverage falls below its target and liquidity becomes strained.

ORSA helps management decide whether to:

- Slow growth
- Change pricing or product design
- Purchase additional reinsurance
- Adjust investments
- Raise capital
- Retain more earnings

A strong current coverage ratio does not by itself establish that the future business plan is sustainable.

## Frequency and reporting

ORSA is performed at least annually and following significant changes in the insurer’s risk profile.

Its findings are documented internally and communicated to the supervisor.

The process should influence actual decisions rather than exist only to satisfy a reporting requirement.
